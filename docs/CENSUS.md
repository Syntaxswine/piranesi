# The census

Generated by `node tools/census.mjs`. **Do not edit by hand** — but do read the
diff, because a change here means the grammar now admits a different set of
blocks than it did.

Every block the grammar admits, walked, measured and ranked. Not a hand dealt
from a seed — `buildCatalog` deals twenty-four of these and the rest had never
been looked at.

## Three numbers

| | count | |
|---|---:|---|
| recipes | 525,056 | every string the grammar admits |
| solids | 262,528 | …that differ as geometry. Material is a skin, so exactly half |
| **blocks** | **66,920** | …that differ once you are allowed to **turn** one |

Rotation is free at placement, so a block and the same block a quarter turn
round are one block. That collapses 262,528 to 66,920 —
**75% of any "every combination" catalogue is the same block relabelled.**

66,816 stacks · 104 arches · from 64 layer tokens
(24 plans × their turns) in 3 layers · a block is 9 yards cubed,
9×9×9 sub-blocks, all curves struck at R 2.5 or R 4.5.

## What is in there

**Mass.** median 49% of the box is stone, mean 50%,
range 11%–100%.

**Ways through** — how many axes you can get from one face to the opposite one
through the void. A vocabulary of nothing but solids is a quarry.

A horizontal way must be **roofed**: you cannot walk through a block by
walking over it. That distinction was found by looking at a picture rather
than a number — the first ranking filled up with big slabs wearing a small
block as a hat, all scoring three ways out of three, because a mostly-empty
top storey connects every face to every other through the open air above the
block. A vertical way is exempt: a shaft cannot be roofed, that is what makes
it a shaft.

## The socket vocabulary

Every block presents a 9×9 pattern of stone on each wall. Two blocks meet
flush when the touching patterns are identical. Across the whole catalogue and
all four turns there are **1,786 distinct wall patterns** —
out of 267680 presented, so the vocabulary is far more concentrated
than the block count suggests, which is why anything meets anything at all.

The solid wall leads, as it should: it is what makes a run of masonry read as
one mass, and it is the reason the owner asked for blocks that are just solid
space. What comes next is the shape of the whole thing — **one storey open, in
each of the three positions, tied at exactly the same count.** The grammar has
no opinion about which floor you leave open, and the slices are what make the
three interchangeable. That tie is the cube law showing up as a number.

## Findings

### The one block that meets nothing

Out of 66,920, exactly **1** presents a wall that no other block can
match on any of its four sides:

> `S:rounded,rounded,rounded:stone`

And it explains itself. `rounded` is the square with all four vertical arrises
struck at R 2.5, so its wall is a curved band that only `rounded` produces —
and because `rounded` is four-fold symmetric there is exactly **one** block made
of nothing else. It can only ever meet itself, and there is no second one to
meet. Every other rounded block has a straight-walled storey somewhere that
gives it a way in.

That is a gap in the vocabulary rather than a defect: a fully rounded pier is a
thing you want, and at present it can only ever stand alone. What would fix it
is a companion plan presenting the rounded band on one side and a slice-plane
wall on the other — the piece that lets a round tower die into a straight one.

### Sealed chambers

**88** blocks contain a void with no way out — unreachable, unlightable,
and pure cost. `S:drum,shaft,full:stone`, `S:notch,frame,notch:stone`, `S:notch,shaft,notch:stone`.

The shape is always the same and it is a nice one: a `drum` is a free-standing
cylinder, a `shaft` is the square with that same cylinder bored out of it. Stack
one on the other and the plug fills the hole from below; put anything solid on
top and the bore becomes a closed column of air in the middle of the masonry.
The sort of thing a full walk turns up and a hand of twenty-four never would.

### A vault runs, but it cannot land

The 104 arches are a **vocabulary island**, and this is the census's most
actionable result. Their best block ranks
10,347 of 66,920;
their median is far below that. The reason is not that they are bad blocks.

An arch presents two different kinds of face. Its **flanks** — the walls
alongside the vault — are met by 506 blocks on average, because below the
springing they are just the pier plan and stacks match that happily. Its **caps**
— the ends of the tunnel — are met by 9.4.

Taking the best-scoring arch, `A:xl:corner/3:stone`, and asking what can meet the
end of it: **13 blocks in the whole grammar, and every one of them is another
arch.**

> `A:xl:corner/1:stone`
> `A:xl:stub/1:stone`
> `A:xl:stub:stone`
> `A:xl:corner-small/1:stone`
> `A:xl:wall/3:stone`

Which is half a success. A row of them cancels its shared caps and reads as one
continuous barrel with no membranes between the bays — that is exactly what
`vault()` tags its end caps for, and it works. But **the tunnel has no ending**.
There is no block in the grammar presenting a spandrel on one face and a wall
on the other, so a vault can run forever or stop in mid-air, and nothing else.

`halfVault` was written to be "the piece that lets a vault die into a wall
instead of stopping in mid-air", but its hand chooses which *spandrel* survives
— both of its caps are still arch profiles. It solves the problem along the
wrong axis. What is missing is a **springer**: a stack-like block carrying the
vault cross-section on one face and a slice-plane wall on the other.

Related, and from the same gap: **0 of the 64 half-arches have nothing in the
grammar that can stand on them** — their top face is half a spandrel and no
block presents its match underneath.

### The rest

- **7,246 (11%) have no way through at all** — walls and lumps. Wanted,
  in that proportion: the owner asked for blocks that are just solid space.
- **37,416 (56%) carry less than half their own upper storeys** where one
  slice hands over to the next. Legal, and this is Piranesi, so an impossibility
  is not a fault — but it should be the player's impossibility, not one the
  generator hands out. `S:notch,drum,notch:stone`, `S:notch,drum,notch/1:stone`, `S:stub,shaft,wall-curve/1:stone`.
- **16,827 (25%) offer nowhere to hang a chain.** Anchor sites need
  stone immediately behind them, so the airier blocks have none.
- **0 blocks share a footprint with another** (0 groups) — so at this
  resolution every block in the grammar is distinguishable from every other.

## The most viable

`0.30·reach + 0.14·deck + 0.28·way + 0.12·anchor + 0.16·sound`, scaled down for
a block too thin to be masonry. It is a judgement, so it is weighted in the
open and every part is in the table beside it — argue with the weights, not the
numbers.

- **reach** — how many blocks meet its walls exactly, of a possible
  4171. Its faces being the common currency is what makes it
  worth reaching for
- **deck** — the same question upward: can it join a vertical run
- **way** — how many axes you can get through it on
- **anchor** — has it somewhere to hang a chain
- **stand** — how much of it is carried where a slice hands over to the next

**The first version of this score was wrong in a way worth recording**, because
it is the whole reason for running a census rather than reasoning about one. It
scored a block on whether its walls meet other blocks, whether it stands on
itself and whether it has decks — and 99.8% of the catalogue came back with
four flush walls out of four, *every* block came back fully supported, and 82
tied at a perfect 1.000. The table was sorting alphabetically inside one
enormous tie. That is not really a broken metric, it is a **result**: the cube
law works so well that "does it join" is answered yes for almost everything, so
it cannot separate blocks. The question had to move from *whether* a block's
walls are met to *how common* they are.
